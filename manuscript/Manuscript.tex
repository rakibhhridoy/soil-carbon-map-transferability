\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Global maps of mangrove soil and biomass carbon increasingly underpin carbon-crediting
decisions, yet they are validated almost exclusively with random cross-validation that
cannot test whether a model trained on some regions predicts an \emph{unsampled} one.
Under leave-one-delta-out evaluation across eight mangrove systems, a model scoring
$R^2=0.65$ under random validation collapses to a median $R^2$ of $-1.7$, with every
held-out delta falling outside the model's area of applicability; the collapse holds
across 29 data-driven mangrove regions on six continents. Adding geomorphic and tidal
predictors does not help, and a widely used published soil-carbon map shows a similar
pattern. The breakdown is consistent with delta-specific concept shift; in a parallel
test, terrestrial soil carbon transfers substantially better, so it is not a generic
mapping artifact. In unsampled deltas, 8\% of global mangrove area, the benchmark median
density scales to of order 1.2~Pg CO$_2$e of stock whose out-of-delta error current
products do not report, while ten to twenty-five local
cores recover most of the lost skill.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} blue carbon; mangroves; soil organic carbon;
out-of-distribution generalization; area of applicability; conformal prediction;
spatial cross-validation.

\section{Introduction}
Mangroves are among the most carbon-dense ecosystems on Earth
\citep{donato2011,mcleod2011}, and with seagrasses and tidal marshes form the
``blue carbon'' coastal sinks of disproportionate climate value \citep{fourqurean2012},
storing of order 11.7~Pg of carbon globally, the large majority of it, roughly 10~Pg, belowground in waterlogged, slowly decomposing soils
\citep{kauffman2020}; their per-area carbon density is set by a small number of
geomorphic and climatic controls that vary strongly between settings \citep{rovai2018}.
This soil-dominated stock makes mangroves disproportionately valuable for climate
mitigation and has placed blue carbon at the centre of natural-climate-solution and
crediting policy \citep{macreadie2021,friess2019}. Their continuing loss
\citep{hamilton2018,richards2020netloss} has driven a rapid expansion of wall-to-wall
carbon products: a 30~m global map of mangrove soil carbon \citep{sanderman2018}, a
30~m map of aboveground biomass and canopy height \citep{simard2019}, a global
biophysical typology \citep{worthington2020}, and continental syntheses of stock change
and loss-driven emissions \citep{richards2020netloss,adame2021future}. These products
increasingly underpin operational decisions: setting baselines for blue-carbon credits,
targeting restoration, and informing national plans such as the Bangladesh Delta Plan
2100.

The credibility of those decisions rests on how well the underlying models predict
where they have not been trained, yet this is precisely what conventional validation
does not test. Global blue-carbon maps are almost universally assessed with random or
spatially-pooled cross-validation, which is inflated by spatial autocorrelation:
\citet{ploton2020} showed that for tropical forest biomass a model appearing to
explain more than half of the variance had near-zero skill once spatial dependence was
respected. Mangrove carbon maps have not been stress-tested this way, and the operationally decisive question, can soil-carbon knowledge learned in some deltas
predict an \emph{unsampled} delta, the situation a carbon buyer or a newly-surveyed
coastline actually faces, has not been posed. Answering it requires both an
out-of-distribution evaluation and a way to say, for any location, whether a global
model is even applicable there \citep{meyer2021}, with calibrated uncertainty that is
honest under distribution shift.

Here we address this gap with a multi-delta benchmark and an explicitly
transferability-focused evaluation. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A reproducible \emph{multi-delta benchmark} with frozen leave-one-delta-out
        splits for mangrove total ecosystem carbon (AGB-C + SOC), and a demonstration
        that a widely-used operational product fails the same out-of-delta test.
  \item Three-tier validation, area-of-applicability masking \citep{meyer2021} and
        conformal intervals that expose the spatial-autocorrelation inflation, quantify
        the \emph{transfer gap}, and tell a practitioner where a global model may be used.
  \item Evidence that the failure is \emph{specific to blue carbon}, terrestrial soil
        carbon transfers far better under the identical protocol, and is driven by
        concept shift rather than missing predictors.
  \item A translation of the failure into \emph{carbon-crediting} terms: the stock and
        unreported error at stake in unsampled deltas, and the local-core sample that
        recovers crediting-grade skill.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Standard validation overstates transferable skill}
A gradient-boosting model that explains 65\% of SOC variance under random $k$-fold
cross-validation ($R^2=0.65$) drops to negative skill under spatial-block
cross-validation ($R^2=-0.69$) and to a median leave-one-delta-out $R^2$ of $-1.7$
(Table~\ref{tab:tiers}, Fig.~\ref{fig:tiers}). Because the global $R^2$ confounds two distinct failures, getting a delta's mean level wrong and failing to reproduce its
internal spatial pattern; we separate them (Table~\ref{tab:perdelta}). The level
error is large: per-delta bias reaches $\pm$100--200~Mg\,ha$^{-1}$, so a model trained
elsewhere systematically over- or under-estimates an unsampled delta's stock. More
fundamentally, the model captures essentially \emph{none} of the within-delta spatial
variation: the median within-delta Pearson correlation between predicted and observed
SOC is $r=0.04$ (mean-centred $R^2$ median $=-0.14$), and individual deltas range from
$r=0.76$ (Sundarbans) to $r=-0.52$ (Zambezi). Out of distribution, then, the model
recovers neither the level nor the pattern of soil carbon. The contrast with the random
$k$-fold $r$ ($\sqrt{0.65}=0.81$) is the magnitude by which conventional validation
overstates transferable skill.

\subsection{Every unsampled delta lies outside the area of applicability}
For all eight held-out deltas, \emph{zero} percent of the mangrove area falls inside
the model's area of applicability (Table~\ref{tab:perdelta}; see also Supplementary
Information): each
delta is a separated cluster in covariate space, so every cross-delta prediction is an
extrapolation. Split-conformal intervals calibrated to 90\% marginal coverage on
held-out training residuals realise widely varying empirical coverage across deltas
(0\% for Sundarbans to 90\% for Amazon--Amap\'a; Table~\ref{tab:perdelta}), confirming
that the finite-sample coverage guarantee, which assumes calibration and test data are exchangeable, breaks under cross-delta shift. The AOA result is model-agnostic
and unchanged across the climate, geomorphic, and tidal feature sets.

This zero-coverage result is not a mechanical consequence of having few training deltas.
When instead we hold out a random 20\% of cores drawn from across the (still-seen)
training deltas, 96\% of them fall \emph{inside} the AOA, whereas both spatial-block and
whole-delta holdouts give 0\% (Supplementary Information). The applicability threshold
therefore correctly recognises familiar environment and flags only genuinely novel
locations; that whole deltas are uniformly outside it reflects real separation of deltas
in covariate space, not an artifact of the leave-one-out design.

\subsection{A published global product shows the same per-delta failure}
The preceding results use our own model; to test whether the problem extends to the maps
actually in operational use, we evaluated the published Sanderman et al.\ global mangrove
soil-carbon product (30~m, 0--100~cm) directly against the in-situ cores, delta by delta
(Table~\ref{tab:publishedmap}). The signature is identical: across the 294 cores the map
resolves, the median within-delta correlation is $r=-0.11$ and the median absolute
per-delta bias is 116~Mg\,ha$^{-1}$, with individual deltas mis-estimated by up to
261~Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ (Mekong). The operational product therefore neither reproduces
within-delta pattern nor gets delta means right, exactly the behaviour our
leave-one-delta-out analysis predicts. Two caveats temper but do not overturn this: the
map's circa-2000 epoch does not match every core's sampling date, and because the
product was trained on a global core compilation that likely overlaps ours its agreement
here is, if anything, optimistic. That it still fails per delta makes the point more
forcefully.

\subsection{The collapse holds across 29 regions, not eight deltas}
The eight named deltas are interpretable but few, inviting the concern that the collapse
is an artifact of a small or hand-chosen fold set. To test robustness at scale we set
the named deltas aside and partitioned all 2{,}489 usable mangrove soil cores into
geographic regions by clustering their coordinates (DBSCAN, $\sim$250~km neighbourhood;
Methods), yielding 29 data-driven regions with at least twelve cores each, spread across
six continents. Repeating leave-one-region-out over this larger, geographically balanced
set reproduces the failure (Fig.~\ref{fig:region}, Table~\ref{tab:region}): the median
within-region correlation between predicted and observed soil carbon is $-0.09$, 83\% of
regions have a within-region correlation below 0.2, 90\% have negative $R^2$, and every
one of the 29 regions falls entirely outside the model's area of applicability. In-sample
skill is itself lower on this fully heterogeneous global core set (random-CV $R^2=0.10$
versus 0.65 within the cleaner named-delta subset), but the out-of-region signal is
unchanged in direction and now rests on 29 independent regions rather than eight. The
transfer failure is therefore a property of mangrove systems broadly, not of the
particular deltas we named.

\subsection{Adding geomorphic and tidal forcing does not restore transfer}
A natural objection is that the failure merely reflects missing predictors. We tested
this by augmenting the climate covariates first with geomorphic position
(distance-to-coast and distance-to-river) and then with tidal forcing (mean and spring
tidal range and the tidal form factor, derived from the EOT20 ocean-tide model
\citep{hartdavis2021eot20}), the dominant physical control on mangrove sediment
trapping and carbon burial. The tidal covariates are physically faithful, separating
macrotidal systems (Amazon--Amap\'a, Zambezi, Rufiji; spring range $3.6$--$4.9$~m) from
microtidal ones (Musi--Banyuasin, $0.7$~m). Yet the transfer gap is essentially
unchanged across the three feature sets (gradient-boosting LODO $R^2_{\text{mean}}$ of
$-7.0$, $-5.7$ and $-5.8$ for climate, $+$geomorphic and $+$tidal respectively), and
the AOA-inside fraction remains zero for every delta throughout. The transferability
limit is therefore not an artifact of an incomplete covariate stack along the major
physical axes. Nor is the in-distribution skill an artifact of the GSOCmap prior:
removing it leaves the random-CV $R^2$ unchanged ($<0.005$) and the median LODO $R^2$
nearly so ($<0.2$; Supplementary Information), so the transfer gap does not depend on
that potentially circular feature.

\subsection{Covariate shift does not fully explain which deltas fail}
If transfer failed simply because held-out deltas were environmentally distant, the
\emph{magnitude} of covariate shift should predict the \emph{magnitude} of error. It
does not: across the eight folds, neither the mean dissimilarity index nor the
multivariate energy distance correlates with LODO error in the expected direction
(Spearman $\rho=+0.24$, $p=0.57$ for dissimilarity vs.\ $R^2$; $\rho=-0.38$, $p=0.35$
for energy distance vs.\ RMSE). With only eight folds this test is underpowered, so we
treat it as inconclusive rather than a true null. Instead, the dominant failure mode is
\emph{concept shift}, a change in the environment--carbon relationship itself between deltas, which we demonstrate directly. Fitting a simple model of SOC on four
interpretable standardized covariates (spring tidal range, mean annual temperature,
annual precipitation, distance-to-coast), we compared a specification with shared slopes
and per-delta intercepts (which permits only a shifted mean) against one with per-delta
slopes (which permits the relationship to differ). Under repeated within-delta
cross-validation the per-delta-slope model predicts substantially better (median
$R^2=0.67$ vs $0.43$ for shared slopes), and the fitted slopes flip sign across deltas:
the SOC response to mean annual temperature, for example, is positive in Amazon--Amap\'a
and Rufiji but strongly negative in the Mekong, and distance-to-coast reverses likewise
(Supplementary Information). The environment--carbon
relationship is therefore genuinely delta-specific, so no globally-shared model, however well-resolved its covariates, can transfer across deltas.

\subsection{Aboveground biomass carbon transfers no better than soil carbon}
To test whether the transferability limit is peculiar to soil carbon, we repeated the
entire analysis for aboveground biomass carbon (AGB-C), sampling the 30~m Simard
product within each delta's mangrove mask (500 points per delta) and applying the
identical models, validation tiers, AOA and conformal machinery. The result mirrors the
SOC case (Table~\ref{tab:pooltransfer}): gradient-boosting skill of $R^2=0.64$ under
random cross-validation falls to $+0.12$ under spatial-block CV and to a median of
$-0.60$ under leave-one-delta-out, with every delta again entirely outside the AOA.
That two carbon pools with different measurement principles and spatial structure
fail transfer in the same way strengthens the conclusion that the limit is a property
of the deltas, not of one variable. We note one caveat: because the Simard biomass
product embeds a canopy-height-to-biomass relationship that itself depends on climate,
predicting AGB-C from bioclimatic covariates is partially circular, so the AGB result
is best read as a companion to, rather than an independent replicate of, the SOC
result.

\subsection{Terrestrial soil carbon transfers better}
A sceptic might ask whether any global carbon map fails out-of-region, making the result
unremarkable. As a check, we applied the identical protocol to terrestrial soil organic
carbon, using $\sim$9{,}400 WoSIS profiles (0--30~cm stock; predominantly North American,
with smaller samples on other continents) under a leave-one-continent-out design, and
separately tested the operational GSOCmap product (the FAO Global Soil Organic Carbon
map used for national greenhouse-gas inventories, and a counterpart to the widely-used
SoilGrids predictions \citep{hengl2017,poggio2021soilgrids}) against those profiles per
continent. In this comparison terrestrial soil carbon transferred substantially better
than blue carbon (Table~\ref{tab:terrestrial}): GSOCmap retained a median within-region
correlation of $r=0.40$ with small per-region bias ($\sim$8~Mg\,ha$^{-1}$), and our own
continent-held-out model kept a within-region correlation of $0.35$ with a transfer gap
of only $0.44$ $R^2$ units, versus near-zero correlation and a gap above two for
mangroves. A plausible mechanism is that terrestrial soil carbon is strongly governed by
climate, which varies across continents and therefore generalises, whereas mangrove
deltas share a near-uniform tropical climate and differ instead in delta-specific factors
that do not. We therefore read the blue-carbon failure as unlikely to be a generic
artifact of global mapping, while noting that the terrestrial comparison is geographically
uneven and rests on a single soil-carbon pool.

\subsection{Carbon-crediting baselines in unsampled deltas}
This transfer failure has an accounting implication, which a simple scaling makes
concrete. Of the sixteen mangrove systems we examined, seven sizeable deltas, including
the Niger, Irrawaddy and Indus, together \SI{11724}{\kilo\metre\squared} or 8\% of
global mangrove area, carry no usable in-situ cores and are exactly the setting in which
a project must rely on a global map. At the benchmark median density their combined stock
is of order \SI{322}{\tera\gram}~C (\SI{1.2}{\peta\gram}~CO$_2$e), yet all seven lie
entirely outside the model's area of applicability, and the out-of-delta error from our
leave-one-delta-out analysis ($\sim$\SI{124}{\mega\gram\per\hectare} combined RMSE), if it
applies at this scale, implies an uncertainty of order $\pm$\SI{83}{\tera\gram}~C
($\pm$\SI{0.3}{\peta\gram}~CO$_2$e) (Table~\ref{tab:crediting}), error that the products'
reported confidence intervals do not capture. These figures are order-of-magnitude
illustrations rather than validated estimates, since no in-situ truth exists for these
deltas; the robust point is that crediting baselines built on global maps for unsampled
deltas are unverifiable, all of this area lying outside the area of applicability.

\subsection{A few local cores recover much of the lost skill}
The transferability limit is not a counsel of despair but a quantifiable data
requirement. We measured how quickly skill recovers when a small number of cores from
the otherwise-unsampled target delta are added to training (Fig.~\ref{fig:fewshot},
Table~\ref{tab:fewshot}): for each held-out delta (restricted to the five with enough
cores to spare) we moved $k$ randomly chosen local cores into the training set and
evaluated on the remainder, averaged over repeated draws. The recovery is rapid: the
median within-delta correlation rises from $r=0.14$ at $k=0$ (pure leave-one-delta-out)
to $0.55$ at $k=10$ and $0.67$ at $k=25$, while median RMSE falls from 76 to
62~Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. On the order of ten to twenty-five local cores thus recover most of the
within-delta predictive skill that a purely global model lacks entirely. The curve
quantifies the local-data cost of a crediting-grade estimate in a new delta and
identifies a practical middle path between an untrustworthy global prediction and a full
local inventory.

\section{Discussion}
Our central result is that the apparent accuracy of a global mangrove soil-carbon model
is largely an artifact of in-distribution validation. The same model that explains two
thirds of the variance under random cross-validation cannot predict an unsampled delta
better than its global mean, and--more diagnostically--every held-out delta lies wholly
outside the model's area of applicability. Because this AOA result is model-agnostic and
invariant to the addition of geomorphic and tidal predictors, it is not readily
dismissed as a quirk of one algorithm or a missing covariate. Mangrove systems
occupy separated regions of environmental space, and a model trained on some of them is,
for an unsampled one, always extrapolating, a pattern that holds not only for the eight
named deltas but across 29 data-driven regions spanning six continents.

The practical implication is specific. Wall-to-wall blue-carbon maps are increasingly
used to set baselines for carbon crediting and restoration prioritisation, yet their
reported uncertainties derive from in-distribution validation and from conformal-style
guarantees that, as we show, fail under cross-delta shift (empirical coverage as low as
0\%). A crediting-grade soil-carbon estimate for a delta absent from the training data
therefore cannot be inherited from a global model; it requires local calibration cores.
This is not merely a property of our re-implementation: the published Sanderman et al.\
product, tested against in-situ cores delta by delta, exhibits a similar near-zero
within-delta correlation and large per-delta bias, so the caution plausibly extends to
maps already in use. We note that this product was trained on a global core compilation
that likely overlaps ours and predates many cores in epoch, so its agreement here is, if
anything, optimistic; that it still fails per delta makes the concern more rather than
less compelling.
The same logic identifies where global maps \emph{can} be trusted, inside the AOA, and turns the area of applicability into an actionable map layer for users.

That covariate-shift magnitude does not order the per-delta failures points to concept
shift: the relationship between environment and soil-carbon density itself differs among
deltas, plausibly through delta-specific sediment supply, salinity regime and
hydroperiod that our global covariates only partially capture. The interpretable
dominant shift axes (tidal range in macrotidal deltas, precipitation seasonality in the
Sahelian Saloum, monsoon thermal regime in the Sundarbans) are consistent with a physical rather than a sampling-coverage origin. This suggests that closing the gap will
require not more global covariates but methods that admit region-specific structure, and our few-shot experiment shows the practical version of this directly: introducing on
the order of ten to twenty-five local cores lifts the median within-delta correlation
from $0.14$ to above $0.6$, recovering most of the skill a global model lacks. The
operative recommendation is therefore not to abandon global models but to pair them with
a modest local-calibration sample, and to use the area of applicability to decide where
such local data are needed.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. In-situ soil-carbon labels remain sparse
and unevenly distributed: some folds are small ($n=11$ for the Sundarbans), which
inflates the mean LODO error and motivates our use of the median, and the global core
distribution forced the inclusion of two estuarine systems to reach four continents.
Depth harmonisation to 0--100~cm relies on a small fraction of extrapolated and
organic-matter-inferred values. The AOA threshold is a provisional quantile rather than
a tuned boxplot-whisker rule, and tidal forcing is taken from an empirical tide model
rather than a delta-resolving hydrodynamic simulation. The aboveground-biomass analysis
inherits the Simard product's structure and its partial climate circularity, and
because the AGB and SOC label sets are not co-located, total carbon is combined from
per-delta pool summaries rather than per-pixel sums.

These limitations also define the path forward. The framework extends naturally to
historical extent-change emissions and to sea-level-rise scenarios, where mangrove
persistence itself is increasingly uncertain \citep{lovelock2015,saintilan2020}; co-located
field plots would allow per-pixel total-carbon mapping with jointly propagated
uncertainty; and because every delta is specified only by a bounding box, the entire
pipeline is portable to any coastal system. The contribution of this work is less a new
map than a measurement of how far existing maps can be trusted beyond where they were trained, and a reusable, uncertainty-aware benchmark for improving that reach.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study systems and the delta registry}
Candidate deltas were drawn from the major mangrove systems of the global tropics and
defined by generous geographic windows. To avoid post-hoc selection, inclusion rules
were fixed before any modelling: a system enters the benchmark as a soil-carbon
leave-one-delta-out (LODO) fold if (i) its mangrove area exceeds a minimum threshold,
(ii) it carries at least five usable in-situ soil cores, and (iii) windows do not
overlap, so no pixel is shared between a training and a held-out delta. We further
required the fold set to span at least four continents so that the out-of-distribution
test is environmentally diverse. Mangrove extent was taken from the Global Mangrove
Watch v3 (2020) product \citep{bunting2022gmw3}; each window's extent was clipped and
its area computed in the World Cylindrical Equal Area projection (EPSG:6933) so that
area-by-density stock accounting is unbiased. Systems passing the area but not the
label criterion were retained as prediction-only deltas. Two estuarine systems
(Everglades and an Australian estuary) were added to secure North American and
Oceanian coverage; the final v1 soil-carbon benchmark comprises eight core deltas
across four continents (Table~\ref{tab:deltas}). The complete criteria, thresholds,
and a change log are given in the Supplementary Information.

\subsection{In-situ carbon labels}
Soil organic carbon labels were derived from the Coastal Carbon Network Data Library
v1.6.0 \citep{ccn2024}, retaining cores classified as mangrove habitat with valid
coordinates. For each core we integrated the measured depth series to a standardized
0--100~cm areal stock,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SOC}_{0\text{--}100} \;=\; 100 \sum_i \mathrm{BD}_i \, f_{C,i}\, \Delta z_i
\qquad [\mathrm{Mg\,C\,ha^{-1}}],
\label{eq:soc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{BD}_i$ is dry bulk density (g\,cm$^{-3}$), $f_{C,i}$ the organic carbon
mass fraction, $\Delta z_i$ the thickness (cm) of layer $i$ truncated at 100~cm, and
the factor 100 converts g\,cm$^{-2}$ to Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. Where carbon fraction was
unreported we estimated it from loss-on-ignition organic matter using the median
$f_C/f_{OM}$ ratio of $0.427$ computed over $\sim$12{,}000 paired CCN samples. Layers
with physically implausible values were dropped (bulk density outside
$0.02$--$1.8$~g\,cm$^{-3}$, carbon fraction outside $0.001$--$0.60$). Cores were
required to reach at least 80~cm; the remaining gap to 100~cm was filled with the
deepest valid layer's carbon density and flagged. After a 0.5/99.5 percentile trim for
label hygiene, the procedure yielded 2{,}489 mangrove SOC cores (median
278~Mg\,ha$^{-1}$; 4\% extrapolated, 7\% with carbon inferred from organic matter).
Aboveground biomass labels were obtained from the 30~m Simard et al.\ product
\citep{simard2019,simard2019data} and converted to carbon with a factor of 0.47; AGB
labels were acquired for all eight core deltas and reserved for the total-carbon
analysis.

\subsection{Covariates}
Predictors spanned the major physical controls on mangrove carbon: bioclimate (CHELSA
v2.1 bio1--19; \citealp{karger2017chelsa}), a soil-carbon prior and soil context
(GSOCmap; SoilGrids bulk density and CEC; \citealp{poggio2021soilgrids}), land cover
(Copernicus Global Land Service), geomorphic position (distance to coast and to major
rivers, computed from Natural Earth in equal-area projection), and tidal forcing (mean
and spring tidal range and the tidal form factor, derived from the M2, S2, K1 and O1
constituents of the EOT20 ocean-tide model; \citealp{hartdavis2021eot20}). Gridded
driver layers were sampled at each core in the layer's native coordinate reference
system, with nodata handled per layer; the external driver archive is referenced in
place and not redistributed. The GSOCmap prior is itself a soil-carbon model and is
reported but interpreted cautiously to avoid circularity. Some archived layers were
corrupt or unreadable (SoilGrids texture, OpenLandMap, fine terrain) and were excluded
from the v1 stack.

\subsection{Models and three-tier validation}
We modelled $\log(1+\mathrm{SOC})$ with two estimators of contrasting flexibility: a
ridge-regression baseline (standardized, median-imputed features) and a tree ensemble
(histogram gradient-boosted regression trees \citep{breiman2001,ke2017lightgbm}), the
model family that underpins the operational products tested here, implemented in
scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}. Each was evaluated under three nested validation schemes. Tier~1
is standard random five-fold cross-validation. Tier~2 is spatial-block cross-validation
\citep{roberts2017}, in which whole $5^\circ$ blocks are held out to suppress the
spatial-autocorrelation leakage that inflates random validation \citep{ploton2020};
although the appropriateness of spatial cross-validation for map-accuracy assessment is
debated \citep{wadoux2021}, here it serves as an intermediate diagnostic between random
and out-of-region evaluation rather than as a global accuracy estimate. Tier~3 is
leave-one-delta-out: the model is trained on all but one delta and evaluated on the
held-out delta, the operational analogue of predicting an unsampled system. We summarise the transfer gap as the Tier~1 minus Tier~3 skill, and
report both mean and median LODO $R^2$ together with RMSE and bias back-transformed to
Mg\,ha$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Area of applicability}
For each held-out delta we computed the area of applicability following
\citet{meyer2021}, part of a broader recognition that global machine-learning maps
require explicit applicability assessment \citep{meyer2022}: features are standardized
and weighted by model importance, and the
dissimilarity index (DI) of each test point is its importance-weighted distance to the
nearest training point, normalised by the mean nearest-neighbour distance within the
training data. The applicability threshold is set from the (outlier-aware) upper
quantile of training DI values, and we report the fraction of each held-out delta
falling below it (inside the AOA).

\subsection{Conformal prediction intervals}
To attach calibrated uncertainty we used split-conformal prediction
\citep{vovk2005,romano2019cqr,angelopoulos2023conformal}. A calibration
subset (20\%, minimum 50 cores) was withheld from each LODO training set; the
half-width of the prediction interval was taken as the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the
absolute calibration residuals with $\alpha=0.1$, giving 90\% marginal coverage under
exchangeability. We report the empirical coverage realised on each held-out delta as a
direct test of whether that exchangeability assumption survives cross-delta shift.

\subsection{Transfer diagnostic}
To test whether the magnitude of covariate shift explains which deltas fail, we
quantified each held-out delta's distance from the training feature space two ways: the
mean importance-weighted dissimilarity index used for the AOA, and the multivariate
energy distance between the delta and the pooled remaining deltas on standardized
features (subsampled for tractability). We correlated each shift measure with the
corresponding LODO error (Spearman $\rho$) and, to name the dominant axis of difference
for each delta, ranked covariates by the standardized mean difference between the delta
and the rest. Given only eight folds, these correlations are reported as descriptive
with explicit acknowledgement of low statistical power.

\subsection{Region-scale robustness}
To test the transfer collapse beyond the eight named deltas, we partitioned all 2{,}489
usable mangrove soil cores into geographic regions by density-based clustering of their
coordinates (DBSCAN with a haversine metric, a 250~km neighbourhood radius, and a
five-core density threshold), retaining clusters of at least twelve cores as independent
leave-one-region-out folds. This data-driven definition fixes the number of folds by
where in-situ data actually exist rather than by naming deltas. We then repeated the
gradient-boosting leave-one-region-out analysis, with the identical covariates, model
and area-of-applicability computation, reporting the distribution of within-region
correlation, $R^2$ and AOA-inside fraction over the 29 resulting regions.

\subsection{Terrestrial comparison}
As a specificity control we repeated the analysis for terrestrial soil organic carbon.
From the WoSIS database we computed 0--30~cm SOC stock (concentration $\times$ bulk
density $\times$ thickness) for profiles with the required measurements, assigned each to
a continent, and ran a leave-one-continent-out gradient-boosting model on the CHELSA
covariates. We additionally sampled the operational GSOCmap product at the same profile
locations and evaluated it against the in-situ stocks per continent, the terrestrial
analogue of the published-map test.

\subsection{Carbon-crediting stakes}
For the prediction-only deltas (sufficient mangrove area but no usable cores), we
estimated the stock a global model assigns as their mangrove area times the benchmark
median total-carbon density, and the associated uncertainty as their area times the
median out-of-delta total-carbon RMSE (the soil and biomass LODO RMSE combined in
quadrature), aggregating errors across deltas as independent. Carbon masses were
converted to CO$_2$-equivalent with the factor 44/12.
